Offbeat

Microscopy tasks on engineered heart tissue that three of four AI models got wrong.

  1. TaskHeart tissue grown in a dish beats on its own. An electrical pulse once a second can take over and set the pace, and a tissue that follows it beats exactly once a second. The agent watched videos of nineteen paced tissues and reported which ones were following the pulse.
  2. Why it's hardTwo of the tissues beat every 0.96 seconds, slightly faster than the pulse, so they aren't following it. The difference is small, and an analysis that accepts anything close to once a second gets these two wrong.
  3. ResultOn this task, A3, Claude Opus answered correctly in all three runs and the other models in one run or none. The failed Claude runs had measured the faster beat before calling it captured. A2, the easier companion task, caught three of twelve runs.

01 · The biology

The biology behind the tasks

Engineered heart tissues

Heart muscle cells made from human stem cells are grown into small strips stretched between two flexible pillars. The strips beat on their own, and labs use them to study disease variants and test drugs. Each contraction bends the pillars, and a brightfield camera records it.

Pacing and capture

Labs often pace these tissues with an electrical field at a fixed rate such as once per second (1 Hz). A tissue is captured when every stimulus produces exactly one beat, which locks its beat interval to the stimulus period. A tissue whose own rate is above the pacing rate keeps its own rhythm, and its beats drift ahead of the stimulus.

03 · Second task

Captured or beating on its own?

A2 can be passed by accepting any tissue whose mean rate is within 2 to 3% of 60 bpm. On A3 that rule returns 7 or 8 captured tissues instead of 6, because two of its nineteen tissues beat as steadily as the captured ones, only slightly too fast.

A3Brightfield time-lapse · 19 tissues

Tissues grown from a patient's cells carrying a MYBPC3 variant linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, filmed on day 7 under 1 Hz pacing in three imaging sessions. The pulse itself doesn't appear in the images.

Answer6 captured (Ex00560, Ex00561, Ex00567, Ex00569, Ex00570, Ex00572); the other 13 are not

Ex00575 and Ex00576 beat as regularly as several of the captured tissues, at 0.96 to 0.98 s per beat. Each beat arrives before the next stimulus could have triggered it, so these tissues are running their own rhythm a little faster than the pacing. Every captured interval, timed at the contraction upstroke, sits within 0.010 s of 1.000 s; the closest uncaptured tissue, Ex00575, is 0.024 to 0.035 s short on every interval.

    05 · Development

    Earlier versions

    A1 was the first version of the beat task, with its traps in the signal processing, such as a misleading frame rate. Every Claude run passed it, and GPT-6 Luna missed it in all three runs. B1 and B2 applied the same approach to fluorescence images of sarcomeres. Every model passed both, so they were set aside.

    A1First version · 1 tissue

    What is this tissue's beat rate?

    The tissue is paced at 1 Hz, so the answer is 60 bpm. Three shortcuts give a wrong number. A motion trace has two peaks per beat, one for contraction and one for relaxation, so counting both gives 120 bpm. The tallest peak in the spectrum is the third harmonic, at 180 bpm. The data sheet says 30 frames per second while the file's own timestamps say 32.9, and using the sheet's figure gives 54.5 to 54.7 bpm.

    B1First version · 1 cell

    How long are this cell's sarcomeres?

    The answer is 1.72 µm by two independent methods, within one pixel of the authors' value for this cell. Its myofibrils run at about 44° to the image axes, so measuring along x or y gives 1.91 to 2.39 µm, and an answer left in pixels gives 14.1.

    B2ACTN2-mEGFP confocal · 8 cells

    Which cells have sarcomeres, and how long are they?

    Eight day-32 heart muscle cells from one imaging session, each with its outline. The agent decides which cells have organized sarcomeres, then reports the median sarcomere length across only those cells. Immature cells have no sarcomeres, but a Fourier analysis or a line profile still returns a number for them. Which cells count as striated comes from the dataset's two expert scorers (4 to 5 versus 1 to 2 on their 5-point scale, with no borderline cells), and their calls agree with the authors' classifier.

    06 · Design

    Design rules

    Ground truth

    A tissue paced at 1 Hz has a known period of 1.000 s. The analysis times every beat from the file's own timestamps and holds each interval to that period.

    Where the trap sits

    A wrong frame rate can be caught by careful checking. Recognizing that a tissue beating a few percent faster than its stimulus is not captured takes training in cardiac physiology.

    What the prompt says

    It quotes the depositors' 30 Hz frame rate because that is what anyone opening the deposit would read, and it never names the trap.

    07 · Method

    How the runs were made

    1. Format. Each eval follows the SpatialBench format: a fixed answer block, and notes giving the ground truth, the traps, a separation table and the tolerance rationale. Graded with numeric_tolerance, label_set_jaccard and all_of, checked against the latch-eval-tools grader classes.
    2. Ground truth. Every number in the trap tables comes from ground_truth.py, run on source files that fetch_data.py downloads and verifies by checksum.
    3. Agents. Each model ran in a fresh directory with only the data and shell access to Python: Claude through Claude Code, isolated from any personal configuration, and GPT through the Codex CLI in its workspace sandbox.
    4. Input. A short fixed environment note plus the task prompt was the only input, identical across models.
